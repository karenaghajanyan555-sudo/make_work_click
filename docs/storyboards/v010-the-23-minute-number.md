## 1. Subject and audience
**Who:** freelancers, owners and team leads who work in fragments (our audience). **Pain:**
constant interruptions; they have seen "it takes 23 minutes to refocus" and feel doomed by it.
**Why it works for us:** a number everyone repeats, a source almost nobody has read, a twist in
the source itself, and a one-minute fix backed by a study — knowledge only, no product, no
partner. Topic score (PLAYBOOK § 7, judgement): relevance 4 · problem 3 · usefulness 4 ·
curiosity 5 · demonstration 4 · send 5 · save 4 · freshness 4 · competition 3 · money 1.
Other topics weighed: a meeting-cost meter (overlaps v002's subject; Shopify's calculator is

## 2. What the subject deserves
A number that frightens people turns out, in its author's own words, to have been the good news.
The truthful, vivid way to show it is to *read the evidence with the viewer*: start inside the
number, pull back until it becomes a sentence in a 2006 page, and let her words do the reveal.
The image to remember: a blinking cursor with a small note pinned to it.

## 8. Creative review (Gate 2c, before rendering)
1. *Visually different from the last five?* No to-dos, no workflow window, no switch climax, no
   end card: documents read in close-up on the ink ground; the product world appears only as two
   small windows burying a cursor.
2. *Opening different?* A clock already ticking inside a printed page, pulling back to its source
   — not a headline typing over scattered to-dos.
3. *Right structure?* The subject is a misread claim; an investigation lets the viewer see the
   evidence and reach the correction themselves — the surprise and the credibility.
4. *Strongest moment?* The ink highlight running across her words: "which I guess is not so long."
5. *What viewers learn:* the 23 minutes is time to get back to a task, after ~two other tasks;
   the cost is losing your place; a one-minute ready-to-resume note helps you let go.
6. *Share / save:* send to the colleague who quotes "23 minutes"; save the three-line note.
7. *Unnecessary scene?* S2's paper numbers are the most cuttable; kept as one margin note; cut
   if the voice runs over 36 s.
8. *Ending integrated?* Yes: the note is where the story arrives; the mark is its signature.

## 9. Hypothesis
Skip rate below our first baseline (a moving evidence close-up + a myth hook vs headline
openings); saves and sends per reach above baseline (the note; the "send to the colleague"
trigger); average watch time similar in seconds (longer video). Learn: does an investigative,
low-lime, document-led video hold strangers as well as our product-world videos?

## Risks and open points
- **Naming a real researcher:** quotes are verbatim and attributed; nothing implies she endorses
  us. Typeset, not screenshots; no publication logo.
- Fast Company's 2008 interview (the most-linked source) returned HTTP 403 to every fetch; the
  2006 Gallup Q&A has the same number in her words and is what we cite.
- The interview (23:15, 81.9 %) and the paper (25:26, 77.2 %) report different numbers from the
  same study; we show both with their own labels and claim neither is "the" truth.
- Length ~34 s (longer than our 21–25 s); PLAYBOOK S4 (30–60 s reach peak) is correlation only.

### Trade-offs and choice
- **E1** carries the most evidence (it can even show the 23:15 vs 25:26 discrepancy) and has an
  iconic first object, but the case-file genre is a familiar format, the middle is procedural
  ("exhibit … exhibit …" is predictable), and noir lighting pulls against the brand's calm.
- **E3** is the most daring idea and the only one where the viewer *lives* the finding. But its hook
  depends on sound (muted, the first frame is a page of text), a self-interruption can read as an
  editing error and lose people at 2.3 s, and once the trick resolves (~11 s) the remaining 30 s
  are conventional explanation.
- **E2 is the strongest for this subject.** The metaphor *is* the finding ("harder to reconstruct
  where you were"; we reconstruct it); it works muted; it carries three turns (not so long → it was
  the good news → the last piece is yours) plus a setback (buried at 4/5); the counter gives a
  built-in reason to stay; and pieces clicking into place is the brand's own idea. It is also the
  hardest to render well. It drops E1's 25:26 discrepancy to stay lean.

### What the chosen execution (E2) needs from the engine — for approval with it
1. **Scene type `paper`** (replaces the proposed `evidence`): pieces with seeded procedural torn
   edges and fibres, contact shadows and slight curl, fits on cues, page assembly, burying sheets,
   a perforated note that tears. Model + renderer + registries + catalogue + tests. Touches
   `src/video` + `src/remotion` → every video re-renders once on its next build (pixels expected
   unchanged; posted files stay in `out/<id>/published/`).
2. **Sound:** new effects (room tone, paper flutter/slide, landing/slap, fit, tear) and opt-in
   *silence windows* (music and effects muted over a span). Sourced as CC0 recordings logged in the
   asset ledger, or procedural. Touches MIX_INPUTS → every video's mix re-runs once (bytes expected
   unchanged).
3. **A look test before the build:** stills of frame 0 and the tear. If the torn paper reads as
   flat vector clip-art, stop and rethink rather than ship it.
4. No new dependency, no paid API, renderer/presets/voice/caching untouched.

### Risks (E2)
- The realism of the paper is the whole bet (look test first).
- Text-led frames: Instagram shows mostly-text reels less (PLAYBOOK § 1). Mitigation: macro
  framing, few words per frame, constant physical motion.
- The correction is not new: others already debunk the number (e.g. the cerebra.co.nz article
  above, 2026-09-02). Our edge is her own "good news" line, the execution and the note; we never
  claim to be first.
- Length ~43 s (our videos 21–26 s). If that holds people less well on the stills/voice review,
  line 7 moves to the note's footer (~38 s).
- A real researcher and publication: verbatim quotes, attributed, no logo, no implied endorsement.

## 13. Engineering: adding `paper` without re-keying unrelated videos (investigated 2026-11-18)
**Why my earlier proposal re-keyed everything (verified in code):** a render segment's key hashes
whole directories (`RENDER_INPUTS`: `src/remotion`, `src/video`, `src/brand`, …), and the mix key
hashes `MIX_INPUTS` (`src/sound`, `src/pipeline/sound`, all of `src/video`, …). Adding a scene type
must edit shared files (`src/video/spec.ts` SceneType union, both scene registries; new sounds:
`SfxId`, the effect map in `sfx.ts`), so every video's key changes, even though their pixels and
audio cannot. The same coarse key is most likely why v001–v008 are already stale today (earlier engine changes such as opt-in props; not traced commit by commit).
**Measured exposure today:** only v009 has valid segments (3/3) and a current mix. v001–v008,
`library` and the two orchestrator productions are already stale. Published/approved files
(`out/v008-…/published/`, `out/v003-…/package/`) are never read or written by any cache step,
and delivery is content-addressed (an identical master + mix never re-encodes the upload).
**Proposal (for approval with the visual execution):**
1. *Scoped render keys.* A segment's code hash = engine core + only the scene types that appear in
   that segment. Each scene type's model and renderer live in their own modules (the three inline
   models in `registry.ts` move out); the registries become pure type → module maps and are hashed
   per used entry. Code is hashed with types stripped and whitespace collapsed (a union member or
   type-only edit cannot change pixels). Result: adding `paper` (or any future type, or an opt-in
   module) leaves every unrelated key unchanged; editing a type re-keys only videos that use it;
   editing the core still re-keys all (correct).
2. *Scoped mix keys.* Effects are keyed by the bytes they render (deterministic, milliseconds) for
   the effects a video uses, plus the core mix code; new paper sounds leave existing mixes unchanged.
   Silence windows are an opt-in module used only by specs that ask for them.
3. *One-time carry-over with proof.* Changing the key formula re-keys once by definition. Before
   switching: record every valid cache (today: v009's 3 segments + mix). After: render proof stills
   of v009 at each segment's first/middle/last frame with old and new code and compare PNG bytes;
   re-mix v009 in memory and compare WAV bytes. Identical → the existing segment files and mix are
   renamed/re-keyed, nothing re-renders. Any difference → stop and report, nothing replaced.
4. Tests: a dummy scene type added in a test leaves every existing key unchanged; touching one
   type's module re-keys only its users; touching core re-keys all; the carry-over refuses on any
   byte difference. Orchestrator stage inputs use the same functions.
**Rejected:** a separate bundle/entry for `paper` (zero re-key now, but forks the engine into two
render paths — a duplicate, against the reuse rules); the naive addition (re-keys everything).
**Caveat:** type stripping uses Node's `stripTypeScriptTypes` (Node 22.23, experimental); a Node
upgrade that changes its output re-keys once (never a false cache hit). TypeScript's own transpiler
is the fallback. Order of work after approval: 1–4 (with the proof), then the `paper` scene type
from the look test, then sounds, then spec/voice/stills.
